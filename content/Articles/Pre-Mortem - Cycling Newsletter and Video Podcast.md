*Drafted: June 2026*

---

A pre-mortem flips the usual planning instinct. Instead of asking "how do we succeed?", it asks: **it's 18 months from now and this has failed — what went wrong?** Working backwards from each plausible failure is more honest than projecting optimism forward. The goal is to surface the risks worth taking seriously before they surface themselves.

The context: a newsletter and video podcast targeting US prosumer cyclists, drawing on first-hand endurance riding experience, with reference brands Radavist, DC Rainmaker, and Escape Collective as the competitive landscape.

---

## The Competitive Landscape in Brief

Before the failures, it's worth understanding what you're entering.

**Radavist** is the aesthetics and culture layer of cycling — framebuilder profiles, Beautiful Bicycles, op-eds on what the sport means. Visually stunning, deeply community-rooted, US-centric in scene but global in sensibility.

**DC Rainmaker** owns the obsessive tech review lane — 60-page reviews, hundreds of photos, GPS watches and power meters dissected to the firmware level. A one-person empire built on exhaustive accuracy and trust.

**Escape Collective** is the professional journalism play — no ads, entirely member-funded ($99/year), 110+ articles/month, founded by journalists who left Outside magazine. Breadth, depth, and the full range of cycling from racing to gravel to tech.

The gap in this landscape: none of them are doing **data-driven endurance analysis from a global practitioner perspective**. That's not accidental — it's a narrow lane. But it's a real one.

---

## Failure Mode 1: The Cultural Disconnection Never Closes

**What went wrong:** Content felt like it was written *about* US cycling rather than *from inside* it. References were generic. The audience sensed an outsider narrating their culture from a distance, and trust never formed.

US prosumer cycling culture has specific textures: gravel racing (Unbound, Big Sugar, SBT GRVL), bikepacking routes (GDMBR, Smoke 'n' Fire), domestic racing (Life Time Grand Prix), gear culture around brands like Specialized, Canyon, and Salsa. BRM/randonneuring exists in the US but is a subculture. The mainstream US prosumer is not primarily an audax rider.

**The deeper risk:** trying to mirror the reference brands instead of occupying a distinct position. Attempting to cover what Escape Collective covers, with less resources and less US context, is a losing proposition.

**Mitigations:**
- **Own the outsider angle explicitly.** Frame the perspective as "global endurance rider examining US cycling culture" — not as a deficiency but as the editorial lens. Readers who are tired of US-myopic content will find this refreshing.
- **Lead with universal training science, not local color.** Zone 2 physiology, fueling strategy, cadence efficiency, and mental models for long efforts are geography-agnostic. Your 300 km BRM data and 6 years of Strava analysis are credible anywhere.
- **Build US correspondents or interview subjects into the format early.** A video podcast is a natural vehicle for this — interview US gravel racers, US bikepacking builders, US coaches. They bring the local texture; you bring the analytical frame.
- **Immerse deliberately.** Subscribe to Velonews, follow Life Time Grand Prix closely, join US Strava clubs, attend one major US cycling event in year one if feasible. Cultural fluency accumulates through exposure.

---

## Failure Mode 2: Video Presence Undermines Credibility Before Content Can Build It

**What went wrong:** The video podcast launched before the production quality and on-camera comfort were ready. Early episodes had poor audio, flat energy, and uncertain framing. Prosumer audiences — who consume Escape Collective podcast production and YouTube channels with professional editing — bounced before the content proved itself.

Video is the most unforgiving medium to start in cold. Audio problems signal amateur hour instantly. Poor lighting reads as low-effort. Nervous or halting delivery in early episodes creates a first impression that SEO doesn't fix.

**The deeper risk:** coupling launch to video means the channel that takes longest to learn is the one gating distribution.

**Mitigations:**
- **Launch audio-first, add video later.** A polished podcast with no video outperforms a shaky video podcast. DC Rainmaker built his empire on written text alone for years before adding video. Escape Collective launched as text/newsletter. Add the camera only when the audio and conversation quality is already good.
- **Treat video as a separate skill with a learning budget.** Practice recording 10 episodes that no one sees before recording one that goes public. Review them. Get feedback from someone who will be honest, not kind.
- **Invest in audio before video.** A $100 USB microphone and treated room (bookshelves work) solves 80% of audio quality. Good audio with no video is far preferable to bad audio with a camera.
- **Start as interviewer, not solo presenter.** Interviewing someone gives you a focal point, removes the awkward silence problem, and means the guest's credibility partially carries the episode. Solo monologue hosting is the hardest format and the last one to master.
- **Study the format deliberately.** Pick 10 episodes from cycling podcasts (The Cycling Podcast, Escape Collective, FIT File) and analyze: pacing, question structure, how they handle transitions. This is a skill with learnable mechanics.

---

## Failure Mode 3: Positioning is Too Broad to Build an Audience

**What went wrong:** The newsletter tried to cover too much — gear reviews, race recaps, training advice, culture pieces — without a clear point of view. Readers couldn't explain to a friend what the publication was *for*. Social sharing dried up. The newsletter became one of many, not the one.

Broad positioning is a trap for new entrants. Established players can be broad because they have trust and resources. New publications need a specific promise that a specific reader can identify with.

**Mitigations:**
- **Define the one reader this is for before publishing issue one.** Not "US cyclists" but something like: "The data-curious endurance rider who does 100+ km rides on weekends, uses a power meter or heart rate monitor, and wants to understand *why* their training is working or not." That reader exists in large numbers and is underserved.
- **Your natural lane is already visible in your writing:** analytical, data-grounded, endurance-specialist, personal without being navel-gazing. The Zone 2 article, the 300 km retrospective, the 6-year compounding essay — these have a consistent voice and method. That's your publication's DNA. Don't dilute it chasing broader appeal.
- **Resist gear review territory early.** DC Rainmaker owns that with a decade of trust and exhaustive methodology. Attempting to compete there as a newcomer is a fast path to credibility deficit. Cover gear only when it connects directly to training outcomes or endurance strategy.
- **Give the publication a name and tagline that stakes a position.** "Data-driven endurance cycling for the obsessive amateur" is a position. "A cycling newsletter" is not.

---

## Failure Mode 4: Consistency Collapses at the 90-Day Mark

**What went wrong:** Launch energy was strong. Issues 1–8 were good. Then came a hard training block, a work sprint, a family commitment — and a gap appeared. Then another. Readers who had formed a habit lost it. The algorithm stopped surfacing the YouTube channel. Open rates fell. Resuming felt like starting over.

Newsletter and podcast businesses die more from inconsistency than from bad content. The audience relationship is built on a predictable cadence. Breaking it once is recoverable. Breaking it repeatedly is fatal.

**Mitigations:**
- **Batch produce aggressively in the first 60 days.** Have 8–10 articles written and 4–6 podcast episodes recorded before issue one goes out. A buffer makes consistency survivable through real-life disruptions.
- **Pick a publishing cadence you can sustain in your worst month, not your best.** Bi-weekly is more sustainable than weekly for a side project with real-world constraints. Consistent bi-weekly beats inconsistent weekly every time.
- **Separate production and publishing days.** Don't write and publish same-day. Write on weekends, publish on Tuesdays. The rhythm protects you.
- **Treat the first year as minimum viable consistency.** The goal in year one is not growth — it is showing up. The audience who stays through year one becomes the foundation for year two's growth.

---

## Failure Mode 5: Cold Audience Start Kills Motivation Before Content Proves Itself

**What went wrong:** Publishing excellent content to 80 subscribers for six months is demoralizing. The feedback loop is too slow. The work begins to feel pointless. Production quality drops. Eventually the project is quietly deprioritized.

The cold start problem is real and underestimated. Building an audience from zero in a crowded niche takes longer than it feels like it should.

**Mitigations:**
- **Don't start from zero — borrow audiences.** Guest spots on established US cycling podcasts in the first 90 days. Reddit presence in r/cycling, r/gravelcycling, r/ultraendurance. Twitter/X cycling community (still active and engaged). Strava clubs. These are warm audiences that can convert to subscribers faster than SEO.
- **One shareable piece before launch.** Write one article designed specifically to be shared — data visualization of something counterintuitive about training, a detailed analysis of something the prosumer audience argues about. Seed it in communities. Let it earn inbound subscribers before the newsletter formally launches.
- **Collaborate with US cycling creators early.** Not as competition but as complementary voices. Cross-promotion between newsletters is common and effective in this space.
- **Set a 12-month benchmark, not a 3-month one.** Most content businesses that succeed look like failures at month 3. Commit to the experiment for a full year before drawing conclusions.

---

## Failure Mode 6: Business Model and Role Clarity Not Established Early

**What went wrong:** The "friend's newsletter business" structure was never formalized. Content was produced, audience grew slowly, and then a disagreement about direction, revenue split, or creative control created a rupture that neither the friendship nor the publication survived.

Co-founder / contributor relationships in media businesses are fragile. Incentives must be aligned from the start.

**Mitigations:**
- **Define the arrangement in writing before producing issue one.** Who owns the IP? What is the compensation structure — flat fee per piece, revenue share, equity? Who has final editorial say? What happens if one party wants to exit?
- **Clarify what "success" means for each party.** Your friend may define success as audience growth or ad revenue. You may define it as creative satisfaction or reputation-building. Misaligned success metrics cause friction even when the publication is doing well.
- **Understand the monetization path clearly.** Escape Collective took 2+ years to reach a sustainable membership base. Ad-supported cycling media is extremely competitive. If the business plan relies on either model, the timeline needs to be realistic and the capital to sustain it needs to be available.
- **Protect your independent reputation.** Your byline exists outside this venture too. Ensure you retain rights to republish or reference work you produce.

---

## The Real Asset You're Not Fully Using

The pre-mortem surfaces risks, but it's worth naming the genuine asset that most cycling content creators don't have: **you have six years of quantified endurance data, a 300 km BRM under your legs, and a writing voice that is analytical, personal, and already publication-ready.**

The Zone 2 article, the 300 km retrospective, the compounding consistency essay — these are not "good for a hobbyist." They are good. The writing is clear, the structure is purposeful, and the insights are grounded in real experience.

The risk is not that you lack credibility. The risk is positioning that obscures the specific thing you're credible in. Double down on what your writing already demonstrates: **the endurance practitioner who thinks rigorously about why their body does what it does, over long time horizons.**

That position has an audience in the US. It just needs to find them deliberately.

---

## Summary: The Mitigation Stack

| Risk | Most Likely Cause | Single Most Important Mitigation |
|------|------------------|----------------------------------|
| Cultural disconnection | Trying to mirror US-specific content without local fluency | Own the global analytical lens; interview US practitioners |
| Video kills early credibility | Launching camera before comfort or quality is ready | Launch audio-only; add video in month 6+ |
| No clear positioning | Trying to compete broadly with established outlets | Define one reader precisely; own the data-driven endurance lane |
| Consistency collapse | Real-life disruption meets thin production buffer | Batch 8–10 pieces before launch; publish bi-weekly |
| Cold start demoralizes | Publishing to a tiny audience with slow feedback loops | Guest spots and community seeding before launch |
| Business structure friction | Informal arrangement with a friend under commercial pressure | Written agreement on IP, compensation, and exit before issue one |
